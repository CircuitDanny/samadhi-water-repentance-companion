# Editorial analysis of Scroll One

This analysis records the research decisions behind the reader-facing companion. It is not a replacement for the Fo Guang Shan Hsi Lai Temple liturgy.

## 1. What the document is

The source is the 2022 Fo Guang Shan Hsi Lai Temple edition of *Liturgy of the Samādhi Water Repentance of Kindness and Compassion: Scroll One*. It also includes a Noon Offering Before the Buddhas and a Memorial Altar Dedication.

It is a Chinese Buddhist repentance liturgy, not a single discourse spoken by the historical Buddha. It combines prose teaching, confessions, vows, Buddha-name recitation, prostrations, offerings, mantras, the *Heart Sūtra* and dedication of merit. It is designed for communal chanting under ritual guidance, although a person can also study its teachings privately.

The file contains Chinese characters, Mandarin pinyin and English. It does not contain Vietnamese. Vietnamese names are therefore supplementary context, not source-text translation.

## 2. Tradition and background

The practice belongs to East Asian Mahāyāna Buddhism. In this Fo Guang Shan edition it is especially shaped by Chinese Buddhist liturgy and Humanistic Buddhist practice.

Several strands meet in the text:

| Strand | Evidence in Scroll One | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Mahāyāna bodhisattva practice | bodhi mind, six perfections, universal vows | Reform is undertaken for all beings, not only for private relief. |
| Chinese repentance ritual | confession, prostration, contemplation, vows | A structured way to acknowledge harm and redirect conduct. |
| Flower Adornment or Huayan imagery | Vairocana, Samantabhadra, offerings filling all worlds | Every act is imagined as connected with the whole Dharma realm. |
| Wisdom or Prajñāpāramitā | emptiness, non-arising, *Heart Sūtra* | Harmful actions have consequences, but no permanent stain or fixed offender exists. Change is possible. |
| Pure Land practice | Amitābha, the Western Pure Land, rebirth aspiration | The mind is directed towards favourable conditions for awakening. |
| Chan | meditation language and the closing wish to practise Chan | Repentance includes observing the mind, not only performing ritual actions. |
| Dhāraṇī and mantra | purification and offering formulas | Sacred formulas mark ritual transitions and intentions. |

This combination is normal in Chinese and Vietnamese Mahāyāna practice. Traditions can be distinct without being mutually exclusive.

## 3. Historical interpretation

The two prefaces tell a traditional story about the late Tang monk National Master Wuda Zhixuan. After imperial favour stirred pride, a human-faced tumour appeared on his knee. The tumour identified itself as Yao Cuo, who had pursued Wuda through successive lives because Wuda had been Yuan Yang, the official connected with Yao Cuo's execution. Honoured Kanaka instructed Wuda to wash the tumour with samādhi water, ending the feud. Wuda then composed the repentance text.

The story supplies the ritual's central images:

- water means purification;
- samādhi means a mind that is collected and not carried away by reactions;
- the tumour makes concealed enmity visible;
- confession interrupts retaliation;
- compassion extends from one's own pain to the pain of others.

This should be presented as the liturgy's traditional origin narrative, not as established biography. De Hong's historical study concludes that the author is uncertain and that parts of the attribution were questioned in medieval China. That finding does not remove the story's religious and ethical importance.

## 4. How the practice is structured

| Source pages | Section | What the practitioner is doing |
|---|---|---|
| 3 to 5 | Imperial and ritual prefaces | Hearing why the practice is associated with water, karma and reconciliation. |
| 7 to 10 | Opening praise | Entering a reverent setting and recalling the Wuda narrative. |
| 11 to 15 | Samantabhadra visualisation and universal offering | Expanding a small physical offering into an intention to benefit all beings. |
| 15 to 18 | Prayer of entering repentance | Purifying body, speech and mind, taking refuge and naming the practice's purpose. |
| 18 to 23 | Buddha names and refuge | Placing confession before the Triple Gem and a wider community of awakened witnesses. |
| 23 to 31 | Three obstructions and seven kinds of mind | Establishing the attitudes required for sincere reform. |
| 32 to 42 | Repentance for afflictions | Tracing harmful action back to greed, aversion and ignorance, then making counter-vows. |
| 43 to 50 | Practical reform and further confession | Moving from hidden conduct to accountability, clear intention and sustained practice. |
| 51 to 54 | Mantra, exit prayer and dedication | Closing the scroll and sharing its benefit with living and deceased beings. |
| 55 to 58 | Noon offering | Offering food symbolically to the Triple Gem, sages and beings of the six realms. |
| 59 to 64 | Memorial dedication | Reciting the *Heart Sūtra*, invoking Amitābha and directing merit towards the deceased and all beings. |

The overall movement is:

**prepare → take refuge → acknowledge causes of harm → confess → understand emptiness → vow to change → dedicate the benefit**

## 5. What the major sections mean

### The prefaces

These teach through story rather than abstract doctrine. Wuda's pride makes him vulnerable to an old feud, while earlier kindness creates the condition for help. The narrative expresses the traditional Buddhist view that intentional actions can bear results when supporting conditions gather.

The guide should prevent two misunderstandings. Karma is not a divine punishment, and illness should not be treated as proof of personal guilt. The preface uses illness symbolically and narratively within a traditional karmic worldview. It is not a licence to diagnose another person's suffering.

### Opening praise and visualisation

The practitioner shifts from ordinary activity into ritual attention. Incense represents ethical conduct, concentration and an intention that spreads beyond the individual. Samantabhadra represents vows put into action. The imagined flowers, music and light turn a modest offering into a limitless one.

### Entering repentance

The assembly purifies the three karmas of body, speech and mind, takes refuge in the Triple Gem and admits that confusion has led to harmful action. The language is deliberately collective. Saying “we” reduces self-protection and places the practitioner among all fallible beings.

### Three obstructions

The text arranges the problem in a causal sequence:

1. **Afflictions** are disturbed states such as greed, hatred and delusion.
2. **Karma** is intentional action shaped by those states.
3. **Result** is the suffering or conditioning that follows.

The three reinforce one another. The first scroll concentrates on the obstruction of afflictions. Later scrolls continue with karma and results.

### Seven kinds of mind

These are the inner conditions for useful repentance:

1. remorse and moral shame;
2. concern about consequences;
3. dispassion towards the cycle of compulsive attachment;
4. the resolve to awaken for all beings;
5. impartial care for friends and enemies;
6. gratitude for the Buddha's teaching;
7. insight that a transgression has no fixed, permanent essence.

The seventh point balances the first six. The practitioner takes responsibility without turning an action into an eternal identity. Because actions arise through conditions, new conditions can end the pattern.

### The numerical litany

Pages 34 to 41 recite many numbered doctrinal lists. Their purpose is comprehensive rather than encyclopaedic. The assembly is effectively saying, “Whatever form confusion has taken, whether coarse or subtle, known or forgotten, we acknowledge it.” A beginner does not need to memorise every list during chanting.

The companion should fully explain the lists that directly help practice, especially the three poisons, five coverings, six senses, eight sufferings, ten afflictions, eighteen realms and dependent origination. Highly technical counts such as the eleven biases, twenty-five views of self and ninety-eight afflictions belong in the glossary with a concise description and a note that the source does not enumerate them.

### Hell and judgement imagery

King Yama, wardens and witnesses dramatise moral accountability. In traditional Buddhist cosmology, hells are painful rebirths produced by karma, not eternal sentences imposed by a creator. Many contemporary readers also understand this imagery psychologically, as the way cruelty, fear and concealed guilt can make a mind into a painful world. The guide can present both recognised readings without deciding the matter for the reader.

### Emptiness and the *Heart Sūtra*

“Empty” does not mean nonexistent, meaningless or free of consequences. It means that a person, action or object does not exist through an independent, permanent essence. Everything occurs through changing causes, conditions and parts.

This is essential to repentance. If an offender were permanently bad, reform would be impossible. If an action had no causal reality, responsibility would be meaningless. Emptiness makes change possible while dependent origination explains why conduct still matters.

### Noon offering

Food is offered above to Buddhas, in the middle to sages and below to beings in the six realms. The ritual trains generosity without discrimination. Its dhāraṇīs are sacred formulas associated with transforming and extending the offering. The guide should explain their function rather than invent a word-for-word translation from uncertain ritual Sanskrit.

### Memorial dedication

The practice turns towards Amitābha Buddha and the deceased. The *Heart Sūtra* supplies wisdom; Pure Land aspiration supplies a compassionate direction; dedication prevents the merit from being held as private spiritual property.

## 7. Important figures

The main profiles should be limited to figures who shape the meaning of Scroll One:

- Śākyamuni Buddha, the historical teacher of our world;
- Vairocana Buddha, all-pervading awakened reality in the Flower Adornment tradition;
- Amitābha Buddha, focus of the Pure Land and memorial dedication;
- Medicine Buddha, associated with healing and wholesome conditions;
- Maitreya, the future Buddha and an image of loving-kindness and hope;
- Avalokiteśvara, compassion and the principal figure in the *Heart Sūtra*;
- Mañjuśrī, wisdom;
- Samantabhadra, vows and practice;
- Mahāsthāmaprāpta, collected strength and Buddha-recollection;
- Kṣitigarbha, vows directed towards beings in severe suffering.

The Seven Ancient Buddhas and Buddhas in the repeated litany should receive a compact directory. Where the source provides only a name, the guide must not create a biography or distinctive iconography.

## 8. Pronunciation material

The reader needs practical English cues for frequently spoken names and terms. These should be clearly labelled approximations. A short key can explain long vowels and common consonants. Mandarin pinyin already appears in the source and need not be duplicated throughout the companion.

Vietnamese equivalents are useful only as a bridge for readers who hear names at a Vietnamese temple. A single table is enough, for example Amitābha = A Di Đà, Avalokiteśvara = Quán Thế Âm and Kṣitigarbha = Địa Tạng.

The glossary can then serve its proper purpose as quick reference instead of carrying the whole explanation.

## 10. Redundant or poorly placed material

The source repeats invocations and confessional formulas because repetition is part of ritual practice. The companion should explain a repeated formula once, then point back to it.

The long technical numerical lists interrupt a beginner's understanding when expanded inside the section commentary. Their practical meaning belongs in the main guide; detailed definitions belong in the glossary.

The Wuda story appears in both prefaces and again in the opening praise. The companion should give one clear account, then explain how later allusions reuse its images.

## 11. Useful diagrams

Four diagrams materially improve understanding:

1. the movement through Scroll One;
2. the cycle from ignorance and craving to karma and continued rebirth;
3. the six realms arranged around the point that none is liberation;
4. the six senses as faculty, object and consciousness.

## 12. Useful artwork

The print edition should use a restrained visual system:

- lotus and water ripples for repentance and purification;
- selected public-domain or openly licensed East Asian artworks for major figures;
- a six-tusked white elephant detail for Samantabhadra;
- icon-like diagrams for senses, realms and practice stages;
- no invented portraits for figures whose iconography cannot be securely identified.

All artwork requires a caption and source or licence note.

## 13. Reference strategy

Primary and institutional sources take priority:

- the official Hsi Lai Temple Scroll One PDF;
- Fo Guang Shan International Translation Center publications and glossary;
- Buddhist Text Translation Society or BDK America translations for canonical context;
- SuttaCentral for early Buddhist definitions such as intention and dependent origination;
- academic studies for the history of Chinese repentance ritual;
- museum collection records for artwork.

Contested claims are labelled as such. The guide paraphrases rather than reproduces substantial passages from the copyrighted liturgy.

## 14. Recommended changes to the existing material

- Make the new edition English-first and concise.
- Separate source translation from explanation visually.
- Add source-page references to every section guide.
- Explain the traditional Wuda story while noting the uncertain authorship.
- Put the causal structure of the three obstructions at the centre.
- Give the six senses a full visual explanation.
- Present literal and psychological readings of the six realms as recognised approaches.
- Explain why the Pure Land is not simply another ordinary realm within saṃsāra.
- Explain mantras by ritual function where a secure word-for-word translation is unavailable.
- Retain repeated chants in the source, but do not repeat their explanation.
- Use figure profiles only where the identity and role can be supported.
- Exclude the three original PDFs from a public repository because the publisher reserves reproduction rights. Link to the official edition instead.

## Questions I could not reliably resolve

No personal or temple-specific answer is required to build the guide.

Two scholarly limits should remain visible to readers:

1. The exact authorship and date of the core three-scroll text are uncertain despite the traditional attribution to Wuda Zhixuan.
2. A few ritual invocations, including Precious Uḍumbara Blossom and Pure Cool Ground Bodhisattva-Mahāsattvas, function clearly within the liturgy but do not have securely identifiable individual biographies. They should be explained as ritual images or collective invocations, not presented as documented historical personalities.
